# Data audit: Give Me Some Credit (`cs-training.csv`)

> Generated by `python -m scorecard.data.audit`. Read-only: no data was changed.  
> US consumer-credit data used as a method demo, not an Indian lending model.  
> **Bad** = `SeriousDlqin2yrs = 1`: the borrower went 90+ days past due within 2 years.  
> Caveat: this audit looks at the full file before the train/valid/test split. That is fine for spotting impossible values, but any threshold chosen here should be re-checked on the training split only, so test data does not shape our decisions.

## 1. Shape and dtypes

After dropping the `Unnamed: 0` row-number column: **150,000 rows x 11 columns** (1 target + 10 features).

| column | dtype | non_null | missing | unique |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| SeriousDlqin2yrs | int64 | 150,000 | 0 | 2 |
| RevolvingUtilizationOfUnsecuredLines | float64 | 150,000 | 0 | 125,728 |
| age | int64 | 150,000 | 0 | 86 |
| NumberOfTime30-59DaysPastDueNotWorse | int64 | 150,000 | 0 | 16 |
| DebtRatio | float64 | 150,000 | 0 | 114,194 |
| MonthlyIncome | float64 | 120,269 | 29,731 | 13,594 |
| NumberOfOpenCreditLinesAndLoans | int64 | 150,000 | 0 | 58 |
| NumberOfTimes90DaysLate | int64 | 150,000 | 0 | 19 |
| NumberRealEstateLoansOrLines | int64 | 150,000 | 0 | 28 |
| NumberOfTime60-89DaysPastDueNotWorse | int64 | 150,000 | 0 | 13 |
| NumberOfDependents | float64 | 146,076 | 3,924 | 13 |

**What it means.** Every column is numeric. `MonthlyIncome` and `NumberOfDependents` are `float64` even though dependents is a count: a pandas `int64` column cannot hold `NaN`, so a column with gaps is stored as float. `Unnamed: 0` was just the CSV row number (no customer ID exists), which matters for the duplicates check below.

**Why a bank cares.** The column list and types are a contract between the training data and the scoring API. If a field arrives renamed or as text in production, the model must fail loudly, not score silently with garbage. That is why `load_raw()` rejects any file whose columns differ from the expected list.

## 2. Target balance

| SeriousDlqin2yrs | rows | share |
|---|---|---|
| 0 = good | 139,974 | 93.32% |
| 1 = bad (90+ days late within 2y) | 10,026 | 6.68% |

**What it means.** 6.68% of borrowers went seriously delinquent, about 1 in 15. These are people a lender had already approved, so most are decent risks.

**Why a bank cares.** (a) Accuracy is useless here: a model that calls everyone good is 93.32% accurate and catches zero defaulters, so we use AUC/Gini/KS. (b) The split must be **stratified** so train, validation and test all keep the same bad rate. (c) We should not oversample bads for the scorecard: the score is converted to a probability of default (PD), and PDs feed pricing and provisioning, so they must reflect the true bad rate.

## 3. Missing values

| column | missing | missing_pct | bad_rate_if_missing | bad_rate_if_present |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| MonthlyIncome | 29,731 | 19.82% | 5.61% | 6.95% |
| NumberOfDependents | 3,924 | 2.62% | 4.56% | 6.74% |

**What it means.** Only two columns have gaps. Income is missing for 29,731 borrowers (19.8%) and dependents for 3,924 (2.6%). The gaps are **not random**: borrowers with missing income default *less* (5.61%) than those who reported it (6.95%).

**Why a bank cares.** Missingness is itself information ("did not declare income" is a behaviour). Filling the gap with the median would hide that signal and push 29,731 lower-risk people into a middle-income group with a different risk level. Scorecards usually give "Missing" its own bin so the model learns its own risk weight.

**What it means.** There is no customer ID, so we cannot tell whether a repeated row is the same person entered twice or different people with identical simple profiles. **What it means.** Compare the mean with the median (50%) and the 99th percentile:

| 96 | 5 | 5 | 5 |

**What it means.** Real counts stop at 17, then jump straight to 96 and 98. These appear in 269 rows, and in 269 of them all three columns carry the code at the same time. Being 30-59 days late 98 times inside a 2-year window is impossible, so these are almost certainly **placeholder codes** from the source system, not counts (the dataset documentation does not say what they mean). The rows look alike in other ways too: utilisation is the capped value 0.9999999 in 100% of them, the median number of open credit lines is 0 (vs 8 for everyone else) and the median age is 29. Crucially, their bad rate is **54.6%** vs 6.68% overall.

**Why a bank cares.** Left as numbers, 98 is treated as "extremely late", which happens to correlate with default but for the wrong reason, and it stretches every statistic of these columns. Dropping the rows would throw away a group where more than half default, making the portfolio look safer than it is. The fix has to keep the signal but stop pretending 98 is a count.

**What it means.** Exactly one row has age 0 and no row is aged 1 to 17, so it is a data entry error, not a minor. Ages above 100 are rare but possible. Risk falls steadily with age: 11.1% bad for 18-35 vs 2.0% above 75.

Note this for the modelling stage.

## 8. Revolving utilisation > 1 / > 10 / > 100

| rule | rows | bad_rate |
|---|---|---|
| utilisation > 1 | 3,321 | 37.25% |
| utilisation > 10 | 241 | 7.05% |
| utilisation > 100 | 223 | 4.93% |

| utilisation band | rows | share | bad_rate |
|---|---|---|---|
| <= 0.5 | 108,712 | 72.47% | 2.75% |
| (0.5, 1] | 37,967 | 25.31% | 15.28% |
| (1, 1.5] | 2,721 | 1.81% | 39.73% |
| (1.5, 2] | 229 | 0.15% | 44.54% |
| (2, 10] | 130 | 0.09% | 28.46% |
| (10, 100] | 18 | 0.01% | 33.33% |
| > 100 | 223 | 0.15% | 4.93% |

**What it means.** Utilisation = card balance / credit limit, so the normal range is 0 to 1. Slightly above 1 is possible (over the limit after fees or interest), and those borrowers are among the **riskiest borrowers in the data**: 40% bad between 1 and 2. Above 100 the picture flips: 223 rows with median 2,159, 100% of them whole numbers, and a bad rate close to the population average. These look like dollar balances typed into the ratio field. Between 10 and 100 there are only 18 rows, so the exact cut-off barely matters.

**Why a bank cares.** Utilisation is usually one of the strongest predictors on a scorecard, and a scorecard should show a clean "higher utilisation means higher risk" pattern that credit officers can sign off on. Error rows binned with genuine over-limit borrowers would dilute the riskiest bin and break that pattern.

## 9. DebtRatio when MonthlyIncome is missing

| income | rows | median_debt_ratio | p90_debt_ratio | share_debt_ratio_gt_1 | share_whole_number | bad_rate |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| missing | 29,731 | 1,159 | 3,785 | 93.85% | 100.00% | 5.61% |
| 0 | 1,634 | 930 | 3,588 | 93.08% | 100.00% | 4.04% |
| 1 | 605 | 600 | 1,959 | 92.23% | 51.07% | 2.81% |
| >1 | 118,030 | 0.2912 | 0.7116 | 4.37% | 2.00% | 7.01% |

**What it means.** DebtRatio should be monthly debt payments / monthly income, normally 0 to about 1. That holds when income is a real number (median 0.29). When income is missing, 0 or 1, DebtRatio jumps to medians of 1,159 / 930 / 600, and in the missing-income rows 100% of values are whole numbers. The most likely explanation is that the data provider divided the debt by 1 when income was unavailable, so the column holds the **debt amount in dollars**, not a ratio. Rows with income exactly 1 show the same jump (dividing by 1 changes nothing), which supports this.

**Why a bank cares.** Debt-to-income is a core affordability measure (US ability-to-repay rules lean on it). Mixing dollar amounts with ratios makes "DebtRatio = 2,000" look like a crushing debt burden when it really means "income unknown". The data proves the mix-up hurts: among DebtRatio > 1 rows, the bad rate is 12.8% when income is real but only 5.4% when income is missing/0/1. Two different populations would share one bin.

## 11. Overlap of missing income and missing dependents

| combination | rows | share | bad_rate |
|---|---|---|---|
| income missing / dependents missing | 3,924 | 2.62% | 4.56% |
| income missing / dependents present | 25,807 | 17.20% | 5.77% |
| income present / dependents present | 120,269 | 80.18% | 6.95% |

**What it means.** All 3,924 rows with missing dependents also have missing income (3,924 of 3,924); dependents is never missing on its own. One cause (e.g. an application channel that skipped the household section) explains both gaps. Risk is lowest when both are missing.

**Why a bank cares.** Two missing flags that always move together add no new information, and two correlated variables can make logistic-regression coefficients unstable. The production pipeline will also receive this combination, so it must handle both fields missing at once.

## Issues to resolve before modelling

1. `MonthlyIncome` missing (29,731), plus 0 (1,634) and 1 (605) placeholders.
2. `DebtRatio` is a dollar amount, not a ratio, wherever income is missing/0/1.
3. `NumberOfDependents` missing (3,924), always together with income.
4. Past-due columns: 96/98 placeholder codes in 269 rows (bad rate 54.6%).
5. Utilisation > 100: 223 rows that look like dollar amounts; 18 ambiguous rows between 10 and 100; 1 to 10 looks like genuine over-limit borrowing.
6. One row with age 0.
7. 609 exact duplicate rows with no ID to resolve them.
8. Long tails (income, credit lines, real-estate loans, dependents): rare but plausible.
9. Imbalanced target (6.68% bad).

Cleaning decisions for each issue are recorded in `docs/decisions.md`.
